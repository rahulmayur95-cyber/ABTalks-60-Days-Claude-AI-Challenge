Challenge 01

Headline Detective

The concept Why it matters

Headlines are written to make you click — that's their whole job. Publishers profit from attention, so words like "secretly," "guts," or "furious" often get added to a story that, underneath, is much more ordinary.

In everyday life: the gap between a headline and the article it sits on top of is one of the fastest ways to spot exaggeration — before you've even read a single paragraph.

The concept Why it matters

Before content asks you to think, it often asks you to feel — fear, outrage, envy, urgency. Strong emotion narrows attention and makes people share first and question later.

In everyday life: noticing your own emotional spike while reading is often the clearest signal that a post was built to be shared, not to inform you.

    Three habits for reading media critically

    01

    Read past the headline. Skim the first three paragraphs before reacting or sharing — the nuance usually lives there.

    02

    Name the feeling. If a post makes you feel fear, outrage, or shame within one sentence, pause — that's often the design, not an accident.

    03

    Check who benefits. Ask who profits from you clicking, sharing, or buying before you decide the claim is true.
