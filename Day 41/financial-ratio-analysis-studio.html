Module 01 · Foundational Understanding

What Ratios Are, and What They're Made From

Before calculating anything, you need to know where the numbers live and what a ratio is actually asking.

Why ratios exist

A company's raw financial statements — revenue of $4.2B, assets of $9.1B — are hard to judge in isolation. Is $4.2B in revenue good? It depends entirely on the size of the company producing it. A ratio takes two numbers from the statements and divides one by the other, turning absolute size into a relative, comparable measure — one that can be checked against history, competitors, or industry norms regardless of company size.

Analogy Think of it like miles-per-gallon instead of gallons-used. Knowing a car burned 12 gallons tells you nothing on its own. Knowing it got 45 miles per gallon tells you it's efficient — and it lets you compare a compact car to a truck fairly, even though they carry very different tank sizes.

Where the numbers come from

Every ratio pulls from one (or both) of two core statements:

Balance Sheet = point in time. Income Statement = span of time. Many ratios blend both.

The five ratio families

FamilyQuestion it answersExample
LiquidityCan we pay bills due within a year?Current Ratio
EfficiencyHow well do we use our assets?Inventory Turnover
ProfitabilityHow much profit per dollar of sales/assets/equity?Net Margin, ROE
Leverage / SolvencyHow much debt risk are we carrying?Debt-to-Equity
Market / ValuationHow does the market price our earnings?P/E Ratio (mentioned, covered lightly)

This tutorial builds deep fluency in the first four families — the ones used for internal and credit analysis — and touches market ratios in the final module.

Worked example: reading a mini statement

Example — Northwind Hardware Co.

Accounts Receivable$60,000COGS$300,000
Inventory$100,000Gross Profit$200,000
Current Assets$200,000Operating Expenses$120,000

We'll reuse Northwind throughout this tutorial so the numbers stay familiar as the ratios get more advanced.

Common Misconception "A higher ratio is always better." False for most ratios — a current ratio of 8 doesn't mean great liquidity, it often means idle cash and unused assets. Ratios need a target range, not a maximize-forever mindset.
Practical Exercise Using Northwind's numbers above, calculate Working Capital (Current Assets − Current Liabilities). Then think: is $100,000 of working capital healthy for a $500,000-revenue hardware distributor?
Working Capital = $200,000 − $100,000 = $100,000. For a distributor carrying heavy inventory, this is a reasonable cushion — roughly 20% of annual revenue — but we can't call it "healthy" without comparing it to industry peers, which is exactly what Module 2 teaches.
Key Takeaways
  • Ratios convert absolute numbers into comparable, sized-adjusted measures
  • Balance Sheet = snapshot; Income Statement = period — ratios often combine both
  • Five families: liquidity, efficiency, profitability, leverage, market
  • No ratio is "good" or "bad" in isolation — context (industry, trend, peers) decides that

Module 02 · Applying the Basics

Liquidity & Efficiency Ratios

Can the company survive the next 12 months, and how well does it turn assets into sales?

Liquidity ratios

Current Ratio

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Measures whether short-term assets cover short-term obligations. Northwind: $200,000 ÷ $100,000 = 2.0 — for every $1 of bills due soon, Northwind holds $2 of assets that could cover them.

Quick Ratio (Acid-Test)

Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities

Strips out inventory, the least liquid current asset, because it can't always be sold quickly at full value. Northwind: ($200,000 − $100,000) ÷ $100,000 = 1.0.

Cash Ratio

Cash Ratio = Cash & Equivalents ÷ Current Liabilities

The strictest test — only cash counts. Northwind: $40,000 ÷ $100,000 = 0.4.

Analogy Current, quick, and cash ratios are like checking if you can pay this month's rent using (1) everything you own you could eventually sell, (2) everything except furniture you'd have to haggle over, and (3) only what's already in your checking account. Each is a stricter test of the same question.

Efficiency ratios

Inventory Turnover

Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Inventory

Northwind: $300,000 ÷ $100,000 = 3.0x per year — inventory fully sells through and restocks 3 times a year (about every 122 days).

Days Sales Outstanding (DSO)

DSO = (Accounts Receivable ÷ Revenue) × 365

Northwind: ($60,000 ÷ $500,000) × 365 = ~44 days to collect cash after a sale.

Total Asset Turnover

Asset Turnover = Revenue ÷ Total Assets

Northwind: $500,000 ÷ $450,000 = 1.11x — every $1 of assets generates $1.11 of annual revenue.

RatioNorthwindReads as
Current Ratio2.0Strong short-term coverage
Quick Ratio1.0Adequate even excluding inventory

Common Misconception "Low inventory turnover always means poor management." Not necessarily — some industries (luxury goods, heavy machinery) naturally hold inventory longer. Always benchmark turnover against the specific industry, not a universal number.
Practical Exercise A competitor, Ridgeline Supply, has Current Assets $150,000, Inventory $90,000, Current Liabilities $120,000. Calculate its Current Ratio and Quick Ratio, then compare to Northwind.
Current Ratio = 150,000 ÷ 120,000 = 1.25. Quick Ratio = (150,000 − 90,000) ÷ 120,000 = 0.5. Both are weaker than Northwind's 2.0 and 1.0 — Ridgeline is more inventory-heavy relative to its bills, and would struggle more if it had to pay obligations without selling stock first.
Key Takeaways
  • Current, quick, and cash ratios test liquidity at progressively stricter levels
  • Efficiency ratios (turnover, DSO) reveal how fast assets convert to sales and cash
  • Always benchmark against industry norms, not fixed universal thresholds

Module 03 · Deeper Application

Profitability & Leverage Ratios

How much of every sales dollar becomes profit, and how much risk comes from debt?

Profitability ratios

Gross Margin

Gross Margin = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue

Northwind: $200,000 ÷ $500,000 = 40% — after covering the direct cost of goods, 40 cents of every sales dollar remains.

Operating Margin

Operating Margin = Operating Income ÷ Revenue

Operating Income = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses = $200,000 − $120,000 = $80,000. Margin = $80,000 ÷ $500,000 = 16%.

Net Margin

Net Margin = Net Income ÷ Revenue

Northwind: $60,000 ÷ $500,000 = 12% — the true bottom-line profitability after all costs, interest, and taxes.

Return on Assets (ROA) & Return on Equity (ROE)

ROA = Net Income ÷ Total Assets ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity

Northwind ROA: $60,000 ÷ $450,000 = 13.3%. Northwind ROE: $60,000 ÷ $270,000 = 22.2%.

Analogy Gross, operating, and net margin are like filtering coffee through three finer sieves. Gross margin removes only the biggest grounds (direct product cost). Operating margin filters out running the shop (rent, salaries). Net margin is the final cup — after taxes and interest, the last "pure" liquid you actually keep.

Try it: Margin Waterfall

Each layer strips out another cost bucket — COGS, then operating expenses, then interest & tax.

Leverage / solvency ratios

Debt-to-Equity

D/E = Total Liabilities ÷ Shareholders' Equity

Northwind: $180,000 ÷ $270,000 = 0.67 — for every $1 of equity, the company carries 67 cents of liabilities.

Debt Ratio

Debt Ratio = Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets

Northwind: $180,000 ÷ $450,000 = 0.40 — 40% of assets are financed by debt/liabilities, 60% by equity.

Interest Coverage Ratio

Interest Coverage = EBIT ÷ Interest Expense

If Northwind pays $10,000 in annual interest: $80,000 ÷ $10,000 = 8.0x — operating income covers interest payments 8 times over, a comfortable cushion.

Debt-to-Equity: 0.67 Conservative — equity comfortably outweighs liabilities.
Common Misconception "Zero debt is the ideal capital structure." Not for most companies — some debt is often cheaper than equity and creates a tax shield on interest. The goal is an appropriate level of leverage for the industry and cash flow stability, not the minimum possible.
Practical Exercise A tech company has Net Income $2M, Revenue $20M, Total Assets $15M, Equity $10M. Calculate Net Margin, ROA, and ROE.
Net Margin = 2M ÷ 20M = 10%. ROA = 2M ÷ 15M = 13.3%. ROE = 2M ÷ 10M = 20%. Notice ROE > ROA — that gap is the effect of leverage, which Module 4's DuPont analysis explains precisely.
Key Takeaways
  • Gross → operating → net margin progressively strip out cost layers
  • ROA measures return on all assets; ROE measures return on owners' stake specifically
  • Leverage ratios measure risk from debt, not profitability — high D/E isn't automatically bad
  • Interest coverage shows how safely a company can service its debt from operating income

Module 04 · Mastery & Real-World Application

DuPont Analysis, Benchmarking & Red Flags

Combine everything into the framework professional analysts actually use.

DuPont Analysis: decomposing ROE

ROE can be broken into three drivers, revealing why a company is (or isn't) profitable for its owners:

ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier = (Net Income/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets) × (Assets/Equity)

Northwind: Net Margin 12% × Asset Turnover 1.11x × Equity Multiplier (450,000/270,000 = 1.67) = 22.2% ROE — matching our earlier direct calculation, but now we know it comes from decent margins, efficient asset use, and moderate leverage — not just one factor doing all the work.

Analogy DuPont analysis is like a doctor not just reporting your weight, but explaining it comes from diet, exercise, and metabolism separately. Two companies can have identical 22% ROE for completely different — and differently risky — reasons.

Comparative & trend analysis

A ratio in isolation is only a data point. Real analysis compares it across three dimensions:

Comparison typeWhat it reveals
Trend (time-series)Is the company's own ratio improving or deteriorating year over year?
Cross-sectional (peer)How does it stack up against direct competitors of similar size?
Industry benchmarkIs it in line with sector norms (e.g., retail turns inventory faster than aerospace)?

Northwind's current ratio of 2.0 looks strong alone, but if the hardware distribution industry average is 2.5, Northwind is actually below par — context flips the conclusion.

Red flags & the limits of ratio analysis

  • Rapidly rising DSO alongside flat revenue — customers are struggling to pay, or channel-stuffing is occurring
  • Margins improving while cash flow falls — possible aggressive revenue recognition
  • One-time gains inflating net margin — always check for non-recurring items in the notes
  • Ratios calculated from year-end-only balances can be seasonally distorted — averages are more reliable
  • Ratios say nothing about qualitative factors: management quality, brand strength, litigation risk
Common Misconception "If the ratios look fine, the company is healthy." Ratios are backward-looking accounting snapshots — they can miss off-balance-sheet risk, related-party transactions, and can even be manipulated within accounting rules. Ratios are a starting point for questions, not a final verdict.
Practical Exercise Company A: Net Margin 5%, Asset Turnover 3.0x, Equity Multiplier 1.2x. Company B: Net Margin 15%, Asset Turnover 0.5x, Equity Multiplier 2.5x. Calculate both ROEs — are they equally risky?
Company A ROE = 5% × 3.0 × 1.2 = 18%. Company B ROE = 15% × 0.5 × 2.5 = 18.75% — nearly identical ROE. But Company A earns it through high turnover and low leverage (likely a retailer — lower risk), while Company B relies on high leverage (2.5x multiplier) — a downturn hits Company B's equity much harder. Same ROE, very different risk.
Key Takeaways
  • DuPont analysis splits ROE into margin, efficiency, and leverage — explaining the "why," not just the "what"
  • Always compare ratios across time, peers, and industry — never in isolation
  • Watch for red flags: rising DSO, margin/cash flow divergence, one-time items, seasonal distortion
  • Ratios are a diagnostic starting point, not a complete verdict on a company

Final Challenge

Diagnose a Real Balance Sheet

Capstone Challenge Harborview Textiles reports: Current Assets $320,000, Inventory $180,000, Current Liabilities $200,000, Cash $30,000, Total Assets $900,000, Total Liabilities $500,000, Equity $400,000, Revenue $1,100,000, COGS $750,000, Operating Expenses $260,000, Net Income $60,000.

Calculate: Current Ratio, Quick Ratio, Net Margin, ROE, and Debt-to-Equity — then write one sentence judging its overall financial health.
Current Ratio = 320,000/200,000 = 1.6. Quick Ratio = (320,000−180,000)/200,000 = 0.7. Net Margin = 60,000/1,100,000 = 5.5%. ROE = 60,000/400,000 = 15%. D/E = 500,000/400,000 = 1.25.

Judgment: Harborview has adequate but not strong liquidity (quick ratio below 1 signals reliance on inventory sales to cover short-term bills), thin margins typical of textiles, a respectable 15% ROE, and moderately elevated leverage (D/E 1.25) — worth watching if margins compress further, since debt service would then pressure cash flow.
